# Brief 1: School health clubs and menstrual health in a tribal block

*Illustrative. The programme, the block and every number are invented for
teaching. Annotations in blockquotes use the tags in `../annotation_scheme.md`.*

---

## Summary

Between July 2024 and March 2025, a school health club programme was
implemented in 42 government upper-primary schools in a tribal block of an
eastern Indian state. The programme trained two peer leaders per school and
ran monthly sessions on menstrual health and hygiene. Attendance among girls in
classes 6 to 8 rose from 71 per cent at baseline to 83 per cent at endline, and
the share of girls reporting use of a sanitary product rose from 38 per cent to
64 per cent. The programme reduced menstruation-related absenteeism and
improved hygiene practice.

> **[FRAME]** The title says "menstrual health"; the outcomes are attendance
> and product use. Product use is a reasonable proxy for one part of menstrual
> health. Attendance is not a health outcome, and the summary slides between
> the two without saying so.

> **[CLAIM]** "Reduced" and "improved" are causal verbs. Read on to see whether
> the design earns them.

## Design and data

The programme was delivered in all 42 schools in the block. A baseline survey
was administered to all girls present in classes 6 to 8 in July 2024
(n = 1,914), and an endline survey to all girls present in March 2025
(n = 1,772). Attendance was taken from school registers for the two months
preceding each survey.

> **[DESIGN]** Every school got the programme, so there is no comparison group.
> The only comparison available is before against after, in the same schools,
> across one school year. Anything else that changed between July and March is
> in the estimate. Three things always do: the girls are eight months older,
> the monsoon has ended, and the register is being filled in by teachers who
> know the programme is being evaluated.

> **[SAMPLE]** "All girls present." The endline is 142 girls smaller than the
> baseline. Girls who left school, or were absent on survey day, are absent
> from the endline. If the girls most likely to be absent on any given day are
> also the girls most likely to be absent during menstruation, the endline
> sample is missing exactly the group the programme is about, and the observed
> rise in product use is partly the departure of the girls least likely to
> report it.

> **[MEASURE]** Attendance "from school registers". In many schools the register
> is completed at the end of the week or the month, from memory, and mid-day
> meal reporting gives teachers a reason to record presence generously. The
> two months before baseline fall in the monsoon and the two months before
> endline do not. A seasonal attendance gap of ten points is ordinary in rural
> schools with no programme at all.

## Results

| Indicator | Baseline (Jul 2024) | Endline (Mar 2025) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attendance, classes 6-8 girls | 71% | 83% | +12 pp |
| Reports using a sanitary product | 38% | 64% | +26 pp |
| Reports missing school during last period | 44% | 27% | -17 pp |
| Can name three hygiene practices | 29% | 78% | +49 pp |

Differences are significant at the 1 per cent level (chi-square tests).

> **[ANALYSIS]** A chi-square on pooled girls treats 1,914 and 1,772 as
> independent draws. They are clustered in 42 schools, and the programme was
> delivered at school level. The effective sample for a school-level programme
> is closer to 42 than to 1,900, and a test that ignores that overstates its
> certainty by a wide margin. This is the single most common analytic error in
> programme reports and it is present here.

> **[MEASURE]** "Reports using a sanitary product" and "reports missing school
> during last period" are self-reports collected by the programme's own field
> staff, eight months after the same staff began teaching the girls what the
> right answer is. Some of the 26-point rise is knowledge of what to say. The
> knowledge indicator, which rose 49 points, tells you how much the girls
> learned about the survey.

## Limitations

The study was limited by sample size and by the short duration of the
programme. A longer follow-up would strengthen the findings.

> **[LIMIT]** Neither of the named limitations is the real one. The sample is
> large. The duration is fine for these outcomes. What is missing is a
> comparison group, and the limitations paragraph does not mention it. A
> limitations section that names the weaknesses least damaging to the
> conclusion is a genre, and this is an example of it.

## Conclusion

School health clubs are an effective, low-cost intervention for improving
menstrual health outcomes and reducing absenteeism. Scale-up to the remaining
blocks of the district is recommended.

> **[CLAIM]** "Effective" claims a cause. The design supports: attendance and
> reported product use were higher at endline than at baseline in schools
> that received the programme. Whether the programme is why cannot be known
> from this study. The honest sentence is weaker and still useful: the
> programme was delivered as designed, girls learned the content, and the
> indicators moved in the right direction, which is enough to justify a
> comparison-group study before scale-up, and not enough to justify scale-up.

> **[ETHICS]** Peer leaders are girls aged 11 to 13 trained to run sessions for
> their classmates. The brief does not say what consent process applied to
> them, to the girls surveyed, or to their parents; nor whether a girl could
> decline the session without her absence being noted in a register that the
> evaluation then used as an outcome.

---

*What the annotated reader takes away: a before-after study across a school
year, with self-reported outcomes collected by programme staff and a test that
ignores clustering, can show a lot of movement and establish nothing about the
programme. The fix is a design change, not a bigger sample.*
