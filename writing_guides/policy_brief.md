# Writing a policy brief

A policy brief is two to four pages for a reader who decides things and does
not read reports. It has one message, evidence for it, and one ask.

## The structure

**Title.** The message, as a sentence. "Community early warning reached
nine in ten households; district loss records should confirm it before
scale-up" is a title. "Evaluation of the Community Early Warning Programme"
is a label.

**The message, in the first paragraph.** What was found, how sure you are,
and what should happen. A reader who stops after the first paragraph has the
brief.

**The evidence.** Two or three findings, each a sentence with a number and
its source. One chart or table if it carries a finding better than the
sentence does, and none if not. The design in one clause: "in a survey of
842 households in programme villages" tells a careful reader what kind of
evidence this is.

**What it does not show.** One paragraph. The main limitation and what it
means for the ask. Readers who decide things have seen many briefs that
omitted this paragraph and were wrong, and a brief that includes it is read
as more credible, not less.

**The ask.** One thing, addressed to someone who can do it, with a cost if it
has one. "The Disaster Management Authority should compare 2024 loss records
between programme and non-programme villages before the scale-up decision in
March" is an ask. "Stakeholders should consider replication" is not.

**Where to read more.** The full report, with a link.

## Rules

**One message.** A brief with three messages has none. If the evaluation
found three things, write three briefs or pick the one that matters.

**Numbers with their uncertainty and their base.** "87 per cent (of 842
households in programme villages)" not "87 per cent". "A 31 per cent
increase" should say what the increase is of, over what period, against
what comparison.

**The verb matches the design.** See `evaluation_report_structure.md`. A brief
is where causal verbs most often get attached to non-causal designs, because
the brief is written for people who want a causal answer.

**No jargon the reader would have to look up.** "Difference-in-differences"
becomes "compared with similar schools that did not have the programme".
"Statistically significant" becomes "unlikely to be chance" or is dropped in
favour of the interval.

**Sentence case, plain words, short paragraphs.** A brief is read on a phone
between meetings.

## Length

Two pages is right. Four is the maximum. Six is a report with a different
cover.
