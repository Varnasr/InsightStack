# Writing about uncertainty

Every number in a report is uncertain, and most reports are written as if
none were. The result is that readers cannot tell a finding from a guess,
and learn to treat all numbers as guesses, which is worse for the findings
than for the guesses.

## The three kinds, and how each is written

**Sampling uncertainty.** The number would have been different with a
different sample. Written as an interval: "64 per cent (95 per cent interval
58 to 70)". The interval, not the p-value, is what the reader needs; a
p-value says whether the interval excludes zero, which is one fact about it.

**Design uncertainty.** The number might not mean what the sentence says,
because the comparison cannot rule out other explanations. Written as a
verb choice and a caveat in the same sentence: "product use rose from 38 to
64 per cent among participating girls; without a comparison group the study
cannot say how much of this is the programme". This is the uncertainty
intervals do not capture and it is usually the larger one.

**Measurement uncertainty.** The number might not be the thing it is named
for. Written as a description of what was actually recorded: "girls' own
reports, to programme staff, of product use during their last period".
The reader can then judge the demand effect.

## Sentences that work

"Between 58 and 70 per cent of girls, most likely around 64, reported using
a product."

"The estimate is 18 percentage points. Given the way schools were selected,
the true effect is probably smaller; it is unlikely to be zero."

"We do not know whether the programme caused the change. We know the change
happened in programme schools and we did not measure schools without it."

"This is a local estimate: it applies to households near the eligibility
threshold and may not apply to poorer households further below it."

## Sentences that do not

"The programme significantly improved outcomes." Significant in which sense,
by how much, compared with what.

"Results indicate a positive trend." Every number is a positive trend or a
negative one; the sentence says nothing.

"Further research is needed." Say what research, on what question, and why
the present study could not answer it.

"Robust." The word has a technical meaning (the result survives a specific
alternative specification) and a colloquial one (I am confident), and
readers cannot tell which is meant. Say which specifications were tried.

## Rounding

Report to the precision the data support, which is usually less than the
software prints. A percentage from a sample of 800 is known to about two
points; report "64 per cent", not "64.3". A coefficient with a standard
error of 4.1 is "about 18", not "18.24". Excess digits are a claim to
precision that the interval contradicts.

## The one rule

State the uncertainty in the same sentence as the number, not in a
limitations section three pages later. A reader who takes the number and
leaves the caveat has been given permission to by the layout.
